# Febrile neutropenia: the examination is blind, so the pathway carries the safety

Content version 0.1.0; infectious-disease preview. Sources checked August 28, 2026.
Independent clinical approval remains pending. Educational simulation, not patient care.

## What the learner must distinguish

Neutropenia removes the local signs a clinician normally hunts for. It does not remove the
infection, and it does not blunt the systemic response, which merely lags. A well-appearing
patient with a single fever and a neutrophil count of 0.2 is an emergency, and activation is the
primary recommended action rather than something that follows a diagnosis.

An earlier draft of this scenario said neutropenia means the patient cannot "mount an inflammatory
response." That is wrong and was corrected before authoring. What is blunted is
**neutrophil-dependent and local**: no pus, muted erythema and swelling, often clear imaging, no
pyuria despite infection. Fever, tachycardia, and C-reactive protein still occur. Around three in
five febrile episodes never localize at all, and most of those still respond to antimicrobials,
which is to say they were occult infections rather than absent ones.

The evidence supports those distinctions, not this fictional response curve:

- [NICE CG151](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg151/chapter/recommendations), published
  September 19, 2012. Its key priority for implementation is to treat suspected neutropenic sepsis
  as an acute medical emergency and offer empiric antibiotic therapy **immediately**. Surveillance
  in 2019 and again in 2020 decided not to update it. Note carefully: **CG151 states no hour
  figure.** The familiar United Kingdom "one hour" comes from the sepsis pathway and national
  quality metrics, not from this guideline, and the scenario does not attribute it to CG151.
- [IDSA 2010 update](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/4/e56/382256), *Clin Infect Dis*
  2011;52(4):e56-e93. Still the standing comprehensive IDSA document as of the date consulted.
  It supplies the neutrophil and temperature thresholds and delegates empiric agent selection to
  local policy, which is why the scenario can record intent without naming a drug.
- [ASCO/IDSA 2018 outpatient update](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/fever-and-neutropenia-in-adults-with-cancer/),
  *J Clin Oncol* 2018;36(14):1443-1453. Scope is deliberately narrow: outpatient management of
  low-risk adults. This is the source of the one-hour-from-triage target used in some settings.

## Where the numbers come from

| Value | Presentation | Untreated contrast | After recorded intent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | 38.4 | **36.1** | 37.6 |
| Heart rate | 104 | 128 | 96 |
| Blood pressure | 118/72 | 86/48 | 112/68 |
| Capillary refill | 2 s | 4 s | 2 s |
| Neutrophils | 0.2 | 0.1 | **0.1** |
| White cells | 0.8 | 0.5 | 0.6 |
| C-reactive protein | 42 | 118 | **126** |
| Lactate | 1.8 | 3.9 | 1.9 |

Three values carry the teaching and none is decorative. The untreated temperature **falls** to
36.1, because hypothermia is a recognized deterioration in sepsis and the naive reading of a
falling fever is improvement. The white cell count never rises in any branch, because a patient
with no neutrophils cannot mount a count, and its flatness must not read as evidence against
infection. And the treated C-reactive protein is **higher** than at presentation, because the
marker has a half-life of a day or more and takes roughly ten to twelve hours to move: its climb
after treatment is lag catching up, not failure.

That last point is the most useful biomarker teaching here. C-reactive protein has demonstrated
value at 24, 48, and 72 hours after therapy and essentially none at the door, so a normal or
mildly raised value on arrival is uninformative rather than reassuring. Lactate is the value that
earns its place, since an elevated lactate in a haemodynamically stable neutropenic patient is
associated with later deterioration.

The remaining values are clinically plausible typical ranges rather than guideline thresholds. The
neutrophil count of 0.2 sits below the 0.5 threshold both major guidelines use.

## What is disputed, and stays disputed

**The one-hour target is not a validated biological cliff.** A 2025 systematic review found only
five studies addressing timing in this population specifically, with three showing an association
between delay and mortality and two showing none; where a signal appears it is at two to three
hours rather than at one. A large paediatric target-trial emulation reported an adjusted risk
ratio whose confidence interval crosses one and whose point estimate runs the other way. The
target is best understood as a system-design safety margin, defended precisely because the door
cannot tell you who is on the steep part of the curve.

**Risk scores do not decide treatment.** The established score is validated to identify low risk
of complications in adults with established febrile neutropenia, for the purpose of choosing
outpatient or oral management. The newer score is validated only in clinically stable adults with
solid tumours. Neither is validated in children, neither is validated to decide whether empiric
antimicrobials are given at all, and both are calculated at fever onset without updating. Low-risk
strata still carry real complication rates. Using a score to defer escalation is an off-label use
of the instrument, and the scenario refuses it.

**The guidance itself is dated.** The standing comprehensive document is from 2010, the outpatient
update from 2018, and the United Kingdom guideline from 2012 with two decisions not to update. A
learner should know that.

## What this scenario cannot teach

Empiric regimen choice, de-escalation, duration, antifungal escalation at day four to seven,
growth-factor use, prophylaxis, and source control are all prescribing decisions and are all
absent. So is the antimicrobial-stewardship counterweight, which is real, since a large majority
of suspected presentations turn out not to need ultra-broad-spectrum cover, but which cannot be
separated from agent selection.

The scenario also cannot teach whether this patient survives, or what the source was. Most
episodes never identify one, and the rehearsal ends with the neutropenia continuing, the cultures
pending, and the source unknown, because that is the honest state at handoff.
