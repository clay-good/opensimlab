# Methemoglobinemia with a saturation gap

## Scope and distinctness

This lab rehearses recognition and coordinated escalation when cyanosis and a pulse-coherent low
oxygen-saturation reading do not agree with the supplied arterial oxygen tension after a documented
oxidant exposure. It is distinct from pulse-oximeter artifact, primary pulmonary hypoxemia, carbon
monoxide poisoning, cyanide poisoning, shock, and routine oxygen titration. Toxicology owns the
dyshemoglobin pattern and bounded antidote-intent decision; qualified teams own direct examination,
diagnosis, co-oximeter operation, contraindication review, treatment delivery, rescue alternatives,
and disposition.

## Fixed fixture

- A 42-year-old woman becomes dusky, dyspneic, headachy, and confused 30 minutes after documented
  topical benzocaine exposure during a completed procedure. The supplied monitor shows sinus
  tachycardia 122/min, blood pressure 112/68 mmHg, respiratory rate 26/min, and pulse-coherent SpO2
  85% despite high-concentration oxygen already being delivered by the qualified team.
- A supplied arterial sample is described as chocolate-brown. Its PaO2 is 238 mmHg and its calculated
  oxygen saturation is 99%; multiwavelength co-oximetry reports methemoglobin 32%. These are authored
  teaching values, not learner-acquired tests or a live gas calculation.
- Qualified support, continuous monitoring, oxidant-source cessation, poison-center or medical
  toxicology consultation, and critical-care ownership are already available. Pulmonary, circulatory,
  hemolytic, inherited, medication, and other toxic causes remain open.
- After the learner records bounded qualified-team methylene-blue intent with glucose-6-phosphate
  dehydrogenase deficiency and serotonergic-drug hazards kept explicit, a strict fixed later report
  supplies improved symptoms, heart rate 98/min, and co-oximetry methemoglobin 8%. Conventional pulse
  oximetry remains a secondary trend rather than proof of oxygenation or treatment success.

## Evidence application

The American Heart Association describes acquired methemoglobinemia after oxidant exposure, including
benzocaine, dapsone, phenazopyridine, nitrates, and nitrites. Cyanosis, dyspnea, fatigue, altered mental
status, and discordance between pulse oximetry and arterial oxygen evidence should prompt recognition.
For life-threatening methemoglobinemia, methylene blue should be administered by qualified clinicians;
specialist consultation and rescue options are appropriate when initial therapy is contraindicated or
ineffective. The current US prescribing information contraindicates methylene blue in glucose-6-
phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency because of severe hemolysis risk and carries a boxed warning for
serotonin syndrome with serotonergic drugs and opioids.

Expert consensus identifies blood-gas co-oximetry as the key confirmatory test and warns that ordinary
pulse oximetry becomes unreliable, often trending toward approximately 85% as methemoglobin rises. The
number is not a diagnostic cutoff. Symptoms and urgency depend on the whole patient, comorbidity, anemia,
exposure, and measured methemoglobin rather than a single saturation or percentage.

## Boundaries and hazards

- Exposure, symptoms, blood appearance, PaO2, calculated saturation, co-oximetry, and response are fixed
  teaching states. The engine does not simulate dyshemoglobin chemistry, tissue oxygen delivery, or
  individualized antidote response.
- Controls reconcile, recognize, support and escalate, review supplied co-oximetry and hazards, record
  bounded qualified-team antidote intent, reassess, and hand off. They expose no drug dose, route,
  preparation, infusion, eligibility calculator, or rescue procedure.
- SpO2 near 85%, a saturation gap, or chocolate-brown blood alone does not diagnose methemoglobinemia.
  Direct assessment, multiwavelength co-oximetry, exposure review, and a differential remain necessary.
- The fixed improvement does not prove treatment causality, exclude rebound, hemolysis, serotonin
  syndrome, another dyshemoglobin, ongoing exposure, or another cause, or predict disposition or outcome.
- Real care requires current poison-center or medical-toxicology guidance, local protocols, repeated
  co-oximetry and clinical reassessment, contraindication review, and qualified rescue planning.
