# Pediatric febrile seizure evidence brief

## Decision boundary

This lab rehearses whole-child reconciliation after a brief stopped seizure with fever, recognition of simple features to date, qualified fever-source and serious-illness ownership, parallel infection, recurrence, alternative-cause and caregiver-safety review, serial recovery reassessment, and active-risk handoff. It does not teach active seizure suppression, a fever or seizure medicine recipe, routine testing, or disposition.

“Simple features to date” is intentionally provisional. The child has not yet completed the febrile illness, so recurrence during the same illness remains possible. Fixed reassuring snapshots do not exclude central nervous system infection, sepsis, another dangerous cause, or later deterioration. The lab never calls the event confirmed simple or the illness benign.

## Fixed teaching facts

- A previously well, reportedly developmentally typical 2-year-old boy is 12 kg and 88 cm. Age-appropriate Hib and pneumococcal immunizations are reported current, and no antibiotic has been given.
- After 12 hours of fever, rhinorrhea, and mildly reduced drinking, a first bilateral generalized convulsion lasted about 3 minutes and stopped spontaneously before the learner surface opened. No rescue medicine was given; no focal onset, asymmetry, or recurrence is reported so far.
- He is sleepy and clingy but opens his eyes to caregiver voice, makes eye contact, cries appropriately, and moves and reaches symmetrically while breathing spontaneously. Temperature is 39.0°C, HR 150/min, RR 30/min, BP 94/58 mmHg (MAP 70), clean pulse-coherent room-air SpO₂ 98%, extremities are warm, pulses are normal volume, and capillary refill is 2 seconds.
- No routine glucose or other test is supplied. No nonblanching rash, meningism, bulging fontanelle, persistent focal deficit, respiratory distress, shock, trauma, known ingestion, prior afebrile seizure, or developmental regression is authored. These are snapshots, not exclusions.
- Experienced pediatric, nursing, infection, neurological, airway-capable, and safeguarding teams own examination, fever-source and serious-illness review, locally indicated tests and treatment, recovery and recurrence surveillance, caregiver education, and escalation. The learner selects and delivers none of that care.
- The fixed minute-30 report is awake and interactive, recognizes his caregiver, uses age-appropriate words and play, and moves and reaches symmetrically while remaining mildly tired. No recurrent seizure or focal finding is reported. Temperature is 38.7°C, HR 126/min, RR 24/min, BP 96/60 mmHg (MAP 72), clean pulse-coherent room-air SpO₂ 99%, with warm normal-volume pulses and refill 2 seconds.
- The later report does not prove treatment effect, a fever source, CNS-infection or serious-illness exclusion, confirmed simple classification, durable neurological recovery, recurrence exclusion, discharge readiness, or outcome.

All patient values and timing are fictional face-valid fixtures. They are not predicted fever, seizure, or recovery physiology and must not be copied into clinical care.

## Source synthesis

The current Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne febrile-seizure guideline defines the age, fever, event, recovery, focality and recurrence features used for classification. It emphasizes fever-source assessment, return to neurological baseline, red flags for CNS infection or complex features, targeted rather than routine investigation, management of the underlying fever cause, and caregiver education. Because a simple classification includes no recurrence during the same illness, this lab retains only simple features to date.

NICE NG143 supports first looking for airway, breathing, circulation and consciousness threats, asking whether fever might represent sepsis, using the whole-child serious-illness framework, and safety-netting caregivers. It also states that antipyretics do not prevent febrile convulsions, should address distress rather than temperature alone, and that temperature response must not distinguish serious from non-serious illness.

WHO ETAT Recommendation 3.4 advises against prophylactic intermittent antipyretics, intermittent antiseizure medicines, or continuous antiseizure medicines for febrile seizures. The lab therefore offers no prophylaxis control. WHO's low-resource emergency scope is broader than this stable fictional reassessment and does not validate its values or trajectory.

## Explicit nonclaims

- No real child is examined, monitored, temperature-measured, seizure-timed, tested, diagnosed, treated, counseled, or dispositioned.
- No glucose, urine, blood, culture, lumbar puncture, EEG, ECG, CT, MRI, or other test is acquired or interpreted.
- No antipyretic, antimicrobial, antiseizure or rescue medicine, fluid, oxygen, dose, concentration, route, access, device, airway maneuver, procedure, or treatment is selected or delivered.
- No caregiver communication or seizure-first-aid psychomotor skill is assessed.
- The fixed negative findings and improving report do not exclude CNS infection, sepsis, toxic or metabolic provocation, recurrence, complex evolution, or another dangerous cause.
- The temperature change does not establish treatment effect or distinguish serious from non-serious illness.
- Confirmed simple classification, fever source, illness resolution, epilepsy risk, durable recovery, discharge readiness, disposition, prognosis, recurrence, and outcome remain open.
