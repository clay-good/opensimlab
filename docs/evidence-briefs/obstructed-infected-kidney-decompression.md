# Infected obstructed kidney: drainage is the treatment

Content version 0.1.0; infectious-disease preview. Sources checked August 28, 2026.
Independent clinical approval remains pending. Educational simulation, not patient care.

## What the learner must distinguish

Antimicrobial therapy and source control are separate decisions. A patient on appropriate
antimicrobials who is not improving should prompt a hunt for an undrained source, and an
obstructed infected kidney is that source. The definitive action belongs to someone else, and
so does its timing.

The scenario is deliberately built so the correct answer is not a drug. Its second bounded
decision is a "not now": definitive stone treatment waits until the infection is treated, which
teaches that urgency is source-specific rather than global.

The evidence supports those distinctions, not this fictional response curve:

- [AUA, Surgical Management of Kidney and Ureteral Stones (2026)](https://www.auanet.org/guidelines-and-quality/guidelines/surgical-management-of-kidney-and-ureteral-stones),
  approved November 2025. Statement 38: for adults with obstructing kidney or ureteral stones and
  suspected infection, clinicians should initiate urgent renal drainage, a **Strong Recommendation
  resting on Evidence Level Grade C**. Its sub-statement permits drainage by **either** nephrostomy
  tube **or** ureteral stent, a **Conditional Recommendation on Evidence Level Grade A** — good
  trials showing no meaningful difference. Statement 37 supplies the initial laboratory set, and the
  discussion names both leucocytosis and leucopenia as infection signals while stating that CRP and
  procalcitonin need further research before their clinical utility is established.
- [EAU Guidelines on Urolithiasis (2026)](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/urolithiasis), limited text
  update March 2026. The obstructed kidney with signs of infection is framed as a urological
  emergency. Strong recommendations: decompress urgently by percutaneous drainage or ureteral
  stenting; delay definitive stone treatment until sepsis resolves; re-sample urine after
  decompression; start antibiotics immediately. No numeric hour target is given.
- [NICE NG253](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng253) (published November 19, 2025, replacing
  NG51). 1.11.4 requires the surgical team or interventional radiologist to seek senior advice about
  the timing of intervention and act as soon as possible in line with that advice, which is why this
  scenario places timing with the receiving team rather than the referrer. 1.6.2 and 1.6.5 supply the
  track-and-trigger thresholds and recalculation cadence; 1.6.4 states that lack of improvement since
  a previous score or intervention raises concern independently of the score itself.

| White cells | 18.4 | 22.1 | 16.2 | Clinically plausible, typical range |
| C-reactive protein | 210 | 290 | **268** | Clinically plausible; see below |

The post-decompression C-reactive protein of 268 mg/L is the deliberate teaching value. It is
**higher than the presenting 210** even though every other value has improved, because the marker
has a half-life of roughly 19 hours and peaks near 48. A learner who reads marker direction as the
success signal gets the wrong answer, which is exactly what AUA declines to endorse.

Temperature, white cell count, and the imaging description are clinically plausible rather than
threshold-anchored. The appropriateness of the running antimicrobial therapy is asserted as a
premise of the fiction; the scenario cannot and does not demonstrate it.

## What is disputed, and stays disputed

**There is no hour threshold anywhere.** Two urological bodies make a Strong recommendation to drain
urgently while grading the underlying evidence low, and the sepsis guidance that does supply a
number — early over late source control, ideally within six hours — grades that figure
**conditional on very-low-certainty evidence**, derived from observational studies that most
commonly happened to define "early" as six hours. Strength of recommendation and quality of
evidence point in opposite directions here, and the scenario says so rather than inventing a clock.

**Neither drainage route is correct.** Randomised evidence has not separated percutaneous
nephrostomy from retrograde stenting on clinical outcomes; AUA's Grade A conditional rating reflects
good trials finding no meaningful difference. The lesson refuses any attempt to declare a winner and
places the choice with the qualified team on local resources and patient factors.

**The mortality gradients are observational.** Infected obstruction that is never drained carries
substantially higher mortality, and delay is associated with worse outcomes, but sicker patients are
drained faster and the effect is confounded by indication. No causal effect is claimed.

**One guideline set is currently absent.** The EAU urological-infections urosepsis section is
withdrawn pending review, with an updated version expected in a later edition; in the interim it
points readers to a now-superseded edition of the sepsis guidance. There is presently no single
authoritative urosepsis recommendation set from the urological community.

**The antibiotic evidence base excludes this patient.** The 2025 complicated-urinary-tract-infection
guidance states in its own methods that patients with urinary obstruction, stones, nephrostomy tubes,
and stents were rarely enrolled in the trials, and that outcome data in those groups are very
limited.

Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 full text could not be retrieved directly; its source-control wording,
strength, and certainty were taken from the issuing society's own guideline page and independently
corroborated by search. No SSC antimicrobial-timing figure is cited anywhere in this scenario.

## What this scenario cannot teach

Any procedural competence: nothing about nephrostomy or stent technique, access, anaesthesia,
anticoagulation, or complications. Imaging interpretation, since the CT finding is supplied.
Antimicrobial choice, dosing, or de-escalation, and by extension whether the running therapy was
appropriate at all. Whether decompression would have helped this particular patient, which the
literature itself cannot establish. And triage under resource constraint, which is in practice the
real bottleneck: interventional radiology availability out of hours frequently determines what
actually happens, and differs sharply between settings.

Drainage is not the end of risk. Deterioration after decompression is well described, which is why
this rehearsal ends with the infection unresolved and the stone still in place.
