# Evidence brief: Opioid toxicity

## Intended rehearsal

This intermediate adult emergency-department case rehearses recognizing suspected opioid toxicity
with a definite pulse and severe respiratory depression, supporting ventilation immediately,
recording naloxone intent toward normal breathing, reassessing the initial response, and responding
to recurrent depression with renewed rescue, observation, co-exposure review, and discharge safety.

## Evidence boundary

- The 2025 American Heart Association guideline identifies early recognition, emergency-response
  activation, breaths or ventilations, and opioid antagonists as the mainstay for life-threatening
  opioid poisoning. A patient with a definite pulse and ineffective breathing needs ventilation now.
- Naloxone may take several minutes and repeated doses, so standard airway and breathing support
  continues until the patient is awake and breathing normally. Full arousal is not the treatment goal.
- Opioid effects can outlast naloxone. Adults who respond should be observed in a health care setting
  until recurrent respiratory depression is unlikely, consciousness is normal, and vital signs are normal.
- Repeat opioid-antagonist doses can be beneficial when respiratory depression recurs. Current AHA
  guidance also recommends antagonist supply and use instruction at eventual health care discharge.

## Explicit exclusions

The controls do not examine the patient, confirm a pulse, acquire capnography or glucose, diagnose an
opioid or co-exposure, open an airway, provide oxygen or ventilation, obtain access, select a product,
route or dose, deliver naloxone, model drug kinetics, precipitate or manage withdrawal, treat aspiration
or pulmonary edema, determine observation duration, perform counseling, dispense medication, arrange
treatment, discharge the patient, or assess psychomotor, team, recurrence, disposition, or outcome skill.
