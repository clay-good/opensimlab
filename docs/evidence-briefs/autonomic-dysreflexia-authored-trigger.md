# Autonomic dysreflexia with an authored trigger

## Scope and distinctness

This lab rehearses recognition and immediate coordination of autonomic dysreflexia in a stable adult
with a declared chronic T4 spinal cord injury. It is distinct from Guillain-Barré dysautonomia,
neurogenic shock, essential hypertension, intracranial emergencies, sepsis, and generic urinary or
bowel care. Neurology owns the baseline-relative pressure pattern and bounded trigger transition;
qualified teams own examination, differential diagnosis, invasive catheter care, medication,
complication assessment, and disposition.

## Fixed fixture

- A 36-year-old man has a declared chronic T4 complete spinal cord injury and a verified usual seated
  blood pressure of 98/62 mmHg with heart rate 68/min. He arrives well and conversant.
- After a routine chair transfer, he develops a pounding headache, facial flushing, sweating above
  the lesion, piloerection below it, blood pressure 178/106 mmHg, and sinus bradycardia 48/min.
- The indwelling suprapubic drainage system is declared. A supplied external inspection finds its
  tubing trapped beneath the chair rail with no urine in the bag for 2 hours. The learner does not
  insert, irrigate, replace, or otherwise manipulate a catheter.
- Upright positioning, lowered legs where possible, loosening external constriction, frequent
  pressure and pulse surveillance, and qualified help produce an authored intermediate monitor state
  of 166/98 mmHg and 50/min. This is a discrete teaching calibration, not an individualized effect.
- Releasing the visible external tubing kink produces an authored transition to 124/76 mmHg and
  60/min. At a strict later reassessment, the fixed report is 108/66 mmHg and 64/min with headache
  easing and drainage resumed. Durable resolution, sole causality, complication exclusion, recurrence,
  treatment requirement, disposition, and outcome remain open.

## Evidence application

The Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine guideline describes autonomic dysreflexia in adults with
spinal cord injury at or above T6 as a sudden blood-pressure rise, conventionally more than 20 mmHg
above the person's usual baseline, with possible headache, sweating, flushing, piloerection, and
bradycardia or tachycardia. It recommends immediate upright positioning with legs lowered when
possible, pressure and pulse monitoring every 1–2 minutes, loosening constrictive devices, and a
rapid trigger survey beginning with the urinary system. Bladder distention is the most common
trigger. The guideline uses 150 mmHg systolic as a pragmatic point for qualified consideration of a
rapid-onset, short-duration antihypertensive before potentially provocative bladder procedures, while
explicitly noting that an exact danger threshold is not established. This lab exposes that boundary
as qualified-team work and does not offer medication or catheter-procedure controls.

## Boundaries and hazards

- The lesion, usual pressure, symptoms, rhythm, trigger, pressure and pulse transitions, drainage,
  and later report are authored teaching states, not a validated autonomic physiology model.
- Controls reconcile, recognize, position and support, review a supplied trigger, release one visible
  external kink, reassess, and hand off. They do not perform catheter care or prescribe treatment.
- A pressure rise of more than 20 mmHg above baseline supports recognition in this declared fixture;
  it does not diagnose every hypertensive episode or replace direct assessment and a differential.
- The fixed response after releasing the kink does not prove sole causality, predict another person's
  response, exclude a second trigger, or establish durable resolution.
- Real care requires urgent serial pressure and pulse checks, individualized trigger removal,
  qualified medication and complication assessment when needed, recurrence surveillance, and local
  spinal-injury protocols.
