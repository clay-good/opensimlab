# Carbon monoxide with a reassuring monitor

## Scope and distinctness

This lab rehearses recognition and coordinated escalation when a conventional pulse oximeter looks
reassuring despite a documented shared combustion exposure and neurologic toxicity. It is distinct
from methemoglobinemia, pulse-oximeter artifact, primary pulmonary hypoxemia, smoke-inhalation cyanide
toxicity, syncope without exposure, and routine oxygen titration. Toxicology owns exposure-pattern
recognition and the selected-patient consultation boundary; qualified teams own examination,
diagnosis, co-oximetry, oxygen delivery, hyperbaric selection, transport, and disposition.

## Fixed fixture

- A 36-year-old man and his partner develop headache, nausea, and dizziness after generator exhaust
  accumulates in an attached garage during a power outage. He briefly loses consciousness and remains
  confused after removal. There is no authored fire, smoke inhalation, burn, trauma, seizure, focal
  deficit, chest pain, pulmonary edema, or hypotension.
- Qualified teams have removed him from exposure and are delivering high-concentration oxygen. The
  supplied monitor shows sinus tachycardia 112/min, BP 118/74 mmHg, RR 24/min, and pulse-coherent
  conventional SpO2 99%. That reading is intentionally reassuring and cannot exclude CO poisoning.
- A supplied sample drawn after removal and initial oxygen reports COHb 28%. Glucose is normal and
  ECG shows sinus tachycardia without authored acute ischemic change. Sample timing, prior oxygen,
  syncope, neurologic findings, cardiac findings, co-exposures, and the whole patient remain coupled.
- After qualified hyperbaric-center consultation is recorded as a selected-patient decision, a strict
  fixed later report supplies clearer orientation, easing symptoms, HR 92/min, RR 18/min,
  conventional SpO2 100%, and COHb 7%.

## Evidence application

CDC guidance bases diagnosis on suggestive exposure and physical findings with confirmatory testing.
It specifically warns that conventional two-wavelength pulse oximetry is inaccurate when COHb is
present, that venous or arterial multiwavelength co-oximetry may be used, that elapsed time after
leaving the exposure changes COHb usefulness, and that COHb level does not correlate well with
severity, outcome, or response. It calls for prompt high-concentration oxygen, serial neurologic
assessment, cardiac evaluation in severe poisoning, and counseling about delayed neurologic
complications and follow-up.

CDC lists neurologic impairment, cardiac involvement, severe acidosis, and transient or prolonged
unconsciousness among reasons to consider hyperbaric oxygen, with COHb only one contextual factor.
ACEP's 2025 systematic clinical policy found no Level A or B recommendation. Its Level C statement is
that selected symptomatic patients may benefit based on symptom severity and availability, including
distance and time. The lab therefore records consultation, not a universal threshold or treatment.

## Boundaries and hazards

- Exposure, symptoms, conventional SpO2, co-oximetry, ECG summary, and later response are authored.
  The engine does not simulate CO uptake, tissue oxygen delivery, elimination, or treatment response.
- Controls reconcile, recognize, activate source and patient safety, review supplied severity
  evidence, record selected-patient consultation, reassess, and hand off. They expose no oxygen
  interface or setting, chamber parameters, eligibility calculator, transfer destination, or treatment.
- A normal conventional SpO2 cannot exclude CO poisoning. One COHb value cannot independently diagnose
  the exposure, grade severity, select hyperbaric treatment, or predict outcome.
- The fixed improvement does not prove treatment causality, hyperbaric benefit, complete clearance,
  durable neurologic recovery, cardiac safety, safe disposition, or outcome.
- Real care requires source safety, assessment of every exposed person, direct serial neurologic and
  cardiac assessment, verified co-oximetry with timing, poison-center or medical-toxicology guidance,
  local hyperbaric capability review, and explicit delayed-complication follow-up.
