# Endocarditis: the antimicrobials are working and the patient is dying

Content version 0.1.0; infectious-disease preview. Sources checked August 28, 2026.
Independent clinical approval remains pending. Educational simulation, not patient care.

## What the learner must distinguish

The infection is responding. The valve is being destroyed at the same time. These are separate
problems on separate clocks, and the clock that matters here is surgical rather than antimicrobial.
Nothing medical closes the gap, which is why the correct answer to "what does this patient need" is
a team and a theatre rather than a different drug.

That makes this a third distinct lesson. Meningococcal sepsis taught the speed of a decision the
learner makes. The obstructed kidney taught source control as a second axis alongside antimicrobials.
This teaches a patient already on correct antimicrobials deteriorating from mechanical failure that
no antimicrobial addresses.

The evidence supports those distinctions, not this fictional response curve:

- [2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of endocarditis](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/39/3948/7243107),
  *Eur Heart J* 2023;44(39):3948-4042, with a corrigendum at *Eur Heart J* 2025;46(11):1082. The
  multidisciplinary **Endocarditis Team** and referral to a **Heart Valve Centre** are a named,
  formal structure. Surgical timing tiers: **emergency, under 24 hours**, for regurgitation or
  obstruction causing refractory pulmonary oedema or cardiogenic shock; **urgent, about 3 to 5
  days**, for heart failure or locally uncontrolled infection. Vegetation at or above 10 mm supports
  surgery **together with** an embolic episode or another indication.
- [2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/77/4/518/7151107),
  *Clin Infect Dis* 2023;77(4):518-526, the current diagnostic framework. The diagnosis in this
  scenario is already made, which is what frees the lesson to be about mechanical failure.
- [AHA, Infective Endocarditis in Adults](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0000000000000296),
  *Circulation* 2015;132:1435-1486. Still the standing United States statement as of the date
  consulted, and it predates both the 2023 European guidance and the 2023 diagnostic criteria. That
  divergence is itself worth knowing.

## Where the numbers come from

| Value | Day 3, presenting | After the authored decompensation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart rate | 118 | 132 |
| Blood pressure | 104/62 | 96/78 |
| **Pulse pressure** | **42** | **18** |
| Respiratory rate | 26 | 36 |
| Oxygen saturation | 92% in air | 84% on 15 L |
| Lactate | 2.1 | 4.3 |
| **C-reactive protein** | **180, falling** | **128, falling further** |
| Blood cultures | no growth | no growth |
| White cells | 16.8 | 14.2 |

Two things carry the teaching. The **pulse pressure narrows** from 42 to 18 rather than widening,
because acute severe regurgitation gives the ventricle no time to dilate; the collapsing pulse and
wide pulse pressure of the textbook belong to chronic disease, and the murmur in acute disease is
short, quiet, and easily missed, often with a soft or absent first heart sound. And the **marker
falls while the patient collapses**: a learner reading infection numbers as patient numbers gets the
wrong answer, which is the single commonest error this scenario targets.

The vegetation is 12 mm, which is above the threshold that appears in the guidance. The scenario
refuses to let that stand alone as the surgical trigger, because the threshold operates together
with an embolic episode or another indication, and this patient already has the stronger indication
in front of the learner: heart failure from valve destruction.

Vital signs, laboratory values, and the echocardiographic description are clinically plausible
rather than threshold-anchored. The surgical timing tiers and the vegetation threshold are
guideline-anchored.

## What is disputed, and stays disputed

The surgical timing tiers are **consensus operationalizations of urgency, not thresholds validated
by randomised trial**. The one major randomised trial in this space enrolled a narrow, stable
population selected for large vegetations, and it does not generalise to a decompensating patient.
Optimal timing after a neurologic complication is genuinely unresolved, as is how long antimicrobial
therapy must run before valve implantation, and the management of endocarditis in people who inject
drugs is openly contested on grounds that are partly non-clinical.

Exact recommendation class and level codes for the Endocarditis Team statement and each timing row
were **not independently retrieved** at authoring time, so this brief cites the tiers and the
structure without asserting class letters.

## What this scenario cannot teach

Intraoperative findings, prosthesis choice, and whether this patient survives. Imaging skill, since
the echocardiographic findings are supplied rather than read. Antimicrobial selection or duration,
diuretic and vasoactive management, and ventilatory support, all of which are drug or device
decisions outside the house style.

It also cannot model whether a surgical bed exists. Access differs materially: transfer to a heart
valve centre is a defined network pathway in one setting and an institution-dependent transfer
decision in another, so the scenario names the pathway generically and records the divergence here
rather than teaching one country's route as the route.

The decompensation occurs whatever the learner records, because the treatment is an operation that
happens after this rehearsal. What the learner changes is whether the surgical team is already
engaged when it arrives. The run ends with the surgical decision pending and the outcome unresolved.
