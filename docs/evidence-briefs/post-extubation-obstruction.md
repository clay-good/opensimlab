# Evidence brief: post-extubation obstruction

- **Scope:** Immediate recognition and initial support of adult soft-tissue upper-airway obstruction after tracheal extubation.
- **Target learner:** Anesthesia learners connecting obstructed effort to urgent airway support and reassessment.
- **Last checked:** August 24, 2026.

## Source and decisions

The Difficult Airway Society extubation guideline describes reduced pharyngeal tone as a cause of
post-extubation upper-airway collapse, particularly in patients with obesity or obstructive sleep
apnea. It directs clinicians to confirm airway patency and breathing after tube removal, continue
oxygen, maintain patency with simple airway maneuvers, and use continuous positive airway pressure
when appropriate. The same guideline emphasizes planning, skilled assistance, monitoring, and
escalation when the airway remains compromised (PMID 22321104).

The lesson therefore begins after tube removal with an unsecured facemask airway and a scripted
combination of snoring, paradoxical effort, reduced tidal volume, and reduced capnography. The
accepted initial-response bundle records help escalation, at least 95% oxygen with active positive
pressure, a held jaw thrust, and recovery of modeled gas movement. Soft-tissue obstruction has its
own engine state so the maneuver cannot resolve bronchospasm, and anesthetic deepening remains part
of the separate laryngospasm model.

## Unsafe inference boundary

The fixed severity and recovery rate are teaching values, not clinical thresholds or an individual
prediction. This slice does not measure physical technique, position, consciousness, residual drug
effect, or airway anatomy. It does not model airway adjuncts, laryngospasm, edema, blood or
secretions, aspiration, reintubation, post-obstructive pulmonary edema, recurrent obstruction,
continued monitoring, or team performance. Improvement here cannot exclude those causes or remove
the need for real-world reassessment and escalation.
