# Meningococcal sepsis: recognition is the whole lesson

Content version 0.1.0; infectious-disease preview. Sources checked August 28, 2026.
Independent clinical approval remains pending. Educational simulation, not patient care.

## What the learner must distinguish

A named red-flag sign, a named clock, and a named person to call. Recognition of a strongly
suspected meningococcal pattern is not a diagnosis; activation is not treatment; recorded
qualified-team intent is not a prescription; and telephone ownership is not attendance at the
bedside. The scenario ends with shock unresolved, because correct early action does not
guarantee recovery.

The evidence supports those distinctions, not this fictional response curve:

- [NICE NG240](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng240/chapter/Recommendations) (published
  March 19, 2024): 1.1.9 names the haemorrhagic non-blanching rash with lesions larger than 2 mm
  as a strongly suspected pattern; 1.1.10 forbids ruling out on an absent rash; 1.1.12 notes that
  rashes are harder to detect on brown, black, and tanned skin; 1.2.2 names a senior clinical
  decision maker; 1.2.3 says not to delay transfer to give antimicrobials; 1.5.1 sets the one-hour
  clock from arrival; 1.5.3 lists the blood tests this case supplies; 1.5.4 forbids ruling out on a
  normal C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, or white cell count alone.
- [NICE NG254](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng254) (published November 19, 2025, partially
  replacing NG51): Table 3 supplies the high-risk thresholds this presentation is calibrated to for
  a 12-to-15-year-old; 1.7.6 pairs a fluid bolus with referral to critical care to review the need
  for central access and vasoactive support; 1.7.9 and 1.7.10 justify the surveillance and recorded
  conscious level; 1.7.11 is the scenario's second beat, requiring a consultant to attend in person
  when there is no response within an hour of an intervention; 1.9.2 supplies the United Kingdom
  bolus ceiling that this case reports as regionally contested.
- [Sanchez-Pinto et al., JAMA 2024;331(8):675-686](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2814296):
  the Phoenix criteria supply the scoring bands used to calibrate the two authored states. The
  presentation scores 2 (sepsis, from the coagulation domain) and the untreated ten-minute contrast
  scores 5 (septic shock, adding cardiovascular and neurological points). The score is used to make
  the contrast defensible, not exposed to the learner as a calculator.

| White cells | 3.4 | 2.8 | Deliberate leucopenia, an adverse sign rather than reassurance |

Capillary refill of 4 to 5 seconds is flagged in the limitations register as clinically plausible
rather than threshold-based: NG254 lists a capillary-refill criterion for 5-to-11-year-olds but not
for the 12-to-15 band, so no guideline threshold is claimed for this patient's age.

The patient is MenACWY vaccinated because UKHSA surveillance for the 2024/25 epidemiological year
reports that all 65 invasive cases in 15-to-19-year-olds in England were serogroup B, which that
programme does not cover. A vaccinated adolescent with this pattern is realistic, and the case
refuses vaccination as an exclusion.

## What is disputed, and stays disputed

The one-hour antimicrobial target is contested outside septic shock, though the shape of that
dispute changed in 2026 and this brief was corrected on August 28, 2026 to say so. IDSA declined to
endorse the 2016 and 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign editions, arguing that a universal one-hour
mandate drives antimicrobial use in patients who turn out not to be infected. The 2026 edition
answers that objection with a tiered structure that reserves the one-hour target for septic shock
and for probable or definite sepsis, while permitting a time-limited course of rapid investigation
with a three-hour ceiling where sepsis is only possible. IDSA now lists the 2026 adult and paediatric
guidelines as endorsed. The disagreement was therefore about applying one clock to every
undifferentiated presentation, and it has largely been resolved in favour of not doing that.

None of this weakens the clock in this scenario, and that is the point worth teaching. This case is a
named, high-pretest-probability syndrome with its own one-hour rule from a separate guideline, which
is exactly the population both sides always agreed should be treated immediately. The boundary review
says so rather than generalising the rule to undifferentiated fever.

Fluid ceilings genuinely disagree: the United Kingdom caps a single paediatric bolus well below the
international first-hour ceiling, which for this weight is a large difference. The case presents
that as a fact to review with qualified teams, not a volume for the learner to choose.

Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 statement-level locators were not independently retrieved for this
version, so no statement number from it is cited. NICE NG240 and NG254 recommendation numbers and
the Phoenix bands were checked directly against the sources linked above.

## What this scenario cannot teach

Antimicrobial selection, dosing, or de-escalation; lumbar puncture and neuroimaging timing; source
control and purpura fulminans limb management; vasopressor titration, central access, and
intubation; public-health notification and contact prophylaxis; and whether the patient survives.
Case fatality remains roughly 8 to 15 percent with appropriate treatment, so a simulator that
rewarded correct action with survival would teach a false causal model. This one does not.
