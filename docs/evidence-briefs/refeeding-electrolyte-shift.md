# Weakness after nutrition restarts

Content 0.1.0 is an unsigned, dose-free adult rehearsal of established feeding-associated
electrolyte deterioration. It is not a risk calculator, diagnostic rule, feeding prescription,
or proof that every low phosphate result is refeeding syndrome.

## Sources and disagreement

Checked August 27, 2026; next source review February 27, 2027. This is source checking, not clinical
sign-off. Sources are paraphrased; their tables, dosing schedules, and algorithms are not reproduced.

- [ASPEN 2020, Table 6 and treatment discussion](https://doi.org/10.1002/ncp.10474) supports coordinated
  electrolyte replacement, thiamine, review of all calorie sources, and monitoring. Severe or
  difficult-to-correct abnormalities can justify individualized calorie reduction or interruption.
  The [erratum](https://doi.org/10.1002/ncp.10491) corrects risk tables, not the adult treatment table.
- [AuSPEN 2025, §§3.5.1–3.5.4, 3.6–3.7](https://doi.org/10.1111/1747-0080.70003) cautions against unnecessary
  underfeeding and distinguishes risk from established illness. Severe post-feeding electrolyte
  derangement warrants stabilization before further advancement. Its prevention recommendations
  do not establish a universal treatment feeding rate.

The learner requests an individualized nutrition plan with no further automatic advancement,
including enteral intake, IV dextrose, medication carriers, fluid balance, and ongoing nutritional
needs. This does not secretly select a rate or stop all nutrition. US and GB use the same explicitly
bounded care-intent pathway; regional prescribing equivalence is not claimed.

## Authored contract

A fictional 58-year-old woman, 46 kg and 164 cm, has had negligible intake for 10 days during illness.
Enteral nutrition began 30 hours earlier; thiamine is not documented. She is awake and weak.
Supplied prefeeding phosphate/potassium/magnesium were 1.00/3.8/0.80 mmol/L; current values are
0.30/2.7/0.48 mmol/L. Glucose 126 mg/dL and creatinine 0.8 mg/dL are supplied context, not evolving
models. Alternative causes and renal function still require qualified review.

All subsequent values and times below are fictional contrasts, not expected kinetics or safe waits.
Initial BP is 102/64 mmHg (MAP 77), HR 112/min, RR 22/min, SpO2 97%, and temperature 36.7°C.
Thirty minutes without either partial or comprehensive replacement yields BP 94/58 (MAP 70), HR 124, RR 26 and
private electrolytes 0.22/2.5/0.42. Prompt phosphate-only care produces 0.45/2.7/0.48 after 30 minutes:
it is a valid partial action, not an automatic error or complete rescue. Comprehensive replacement
produces 0.50/3.1/0.60 after 30 minutes with BP 106/66 (MAP 79), HR 100, RR 20. Without a nutrition
plan, the authored incomplete-care branch later returns to 0.35/2.8/0.50 and initial vital signs.
Sixty minutes after both comprehensive replacement and nutrition review produces 0.55/3.3/0.65,
BP 110/68 (MAP 82), HR 94, RR 18. Partial improvement is not normalization or lasting safety.
SpO2, temperature, and awake weakness remain unchanged; there is no predicted arrhythmia or injury.
If phosphate-only care starts after untreated deterioration, potassium and magnesium remain at
2.5 and 0.42; partial care does not reset those unaddressed deficits.

Support, exposure review, monitoring, thiamine, and nutrition review are independent requests, not
administrative gates on urgent electrolyte care. Thiamine creates no invented immediate biochemical
effect. New laboratory findings require explicit reassessment; elapsed clocks reveal no hidden
values. The example pauses before each decision. Automatic feeding advancement and premature
monitoring closure are refused and retained for reflection, but do not prevent later recovery.
Continuing-care handoff requires coordinated care and a fresh combined-response assessment.
