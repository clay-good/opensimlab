# Neonatal tension pneumothorax during respiratory support

## Teaching decision

This preview lesson teaches rapid pattern recognition and escalation, not pleural-drainage technique. A qualified team supplies the examination, device checks, diagnosis, oxygenation and ventilation support, decompression, drain planning, analgesia, imaging, and serial reassessment. The learner only activates help, connects the authored facts, recognizes a suspected tension pneumothorax, reviews qualified-team boundaries, reviews a fixed response, and hands off unresolved risk.

## Evidence boundary

- The 2025 AHA/AAP neonatal-resuscitation guideline makes effective lung ventilation the newborn priority, uses heart-rate rise as the primary marker of effective ventilation, warns that excessive peak pressure and tidal volume can cause harm, and requires close postresuscitation monitoring after prolonged ventilation or advanced resuscitation.
- The Queensland Clinical Guideline for neonatal retrieval stabilization lists increasing respiratory effort, unilateral decreased breath sounds, tachycardia or bradycardia, hypotension, mottling, and increasing oxygen requirement as pneumothorax cues. It treats pneumothorax with significant respiratory distress and cardiopulmonary compromise as an urgent tension-pneumothorax problem.
- The Royal Cornwall Hospitals neonatal pneumothorax guideline describes new or rising oxygen need, increased work of breathing, asymmetric chest findings, and decreased unilateral breath sounds. For an unstable newborn, it says radiography should be deferred and the pneumothorax immediately evacuated.

No source establishes one universal neonatal decompression device, size, site, analgesic plan, ventilator change, or postprocedure trajectory. Those choices depend on local neonatal protocols, equipment, anatomy, expertise, and the newborn's condition. This lesson therefore authors only a qualified-team decompression and a deliberately partial response, with no learner procedure or universal equipment claim.
