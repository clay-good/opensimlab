# Evidence brief: opioid-induced ventilatory impairment

- **Scope:** Early postoperative recognition, ventilatory support, opioid hold, reversal escalation, and reassessment in an adult.
- **Target learner:** Anesthesia learners integrating arousal and ventilation trends before hypoxemia becomes the only signal.
- **Last checked:** August 24, 2026.

## Sources and decisions

The 2021 international consensus statement defines opioid-induced ventilatory impairment as a
combination of central respiratory-drive depression, reduced upper-airway tone, and increased
sedation. It emphasizes repeated sedation assessment, recognizes that respiratory rate and oxygen
saturation are imperfect surrogates, and warns that hypoxemia may appear late when supplemental
oxygen is present (PMID 33027841).

The 2024 BJA Education review identifies naloxone as first-line pharmacologic reversal for serious
opioid ventilatory depression while describing important limitations, including analgesia loss,
withdrawal, short reversal duration, and recurrent depression (PMID 38375496). The browser therefore
records naloxone titration intent but exposes no dose, route, or administration control.

The authored case isolates central-drive depression: spontaneous rate falls much more than breath
size, the airway remains patent, carbon dioxide trends upward, and supplemental oxygen initially
supports saturation. The accepted path recruits help, actively supports ventilation, holds further
opioid, records reversal intent, and checks spontaneous breathing only after delivered breaths stop.

## Unsafe inference boundary

The morphine exposure, arousal finding, severity, and recovery curve are fixed teaching facts. The
simulator does not model morphine pharmacokinetics, arterial blood gas measurement, sedation-scale
technique, pain, upper-airway collapse, co-sedatives, naloxone dose or pharmacology, withdrawal,
analgesia loss, repeated reversal, recurrent depression, or monitoring disposition. Initial
improvement cannot establish that a real patient is safe or that opioid effect has ended.
